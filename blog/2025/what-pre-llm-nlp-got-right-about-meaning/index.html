— and what we lost when we stopped looking at tasks

Bigger language models don’t automatically lead to better understanding, and the rise of LLMs makes tasks, specialized corpora, and human evaluation more important than ever.

Large language models changed how we interact with language.

They generate fluent text, metaphors, poems, explanations — often with a level of surface competence that would have seemed impossible only a few years ago. As a result, our collective understanding of what it means for a system to “understand language” has shifted dramatically.

And yet, something subtle has been lost along the way.

Before LLMs, NLP was not more powerful. But in some important ways, it was clearer about what it was trying to understand. Meaning was not assumed to be an emergent property of scale. It was something that had to be defined, constrained, and tested — often imperfectly, but explicitly.

When NLP was built around tasks

Pre-LLM NLP was structured around tasks. These tasks were sometimes narrow, sometimes artificial, often frustrating — but they forced clarity.

A task required researchers to specify what counted as success, under which conditions, and for which kind of language use. Parsing, similarity judgments, metaphor identification, sentiment evaluation, reading times, and human ratings were not just benchmarks. They were ways of making meaning observable.

Meaning was never taken for granted. It had to be operationalized.

This led to workflows that now look heavy or slow: carefully designed stimuli, controlled datasets, handcrafted features, and human evaluation at every step. But this constraint had an important consequence. Models were never detached from the phenomena they were meant to capture. They were tied — sometimes uncomfortably — to specific questions about human language behavior.

Measuring meaning through human behavior

My own work emerged from that tradition.

In one peer-reviewed study, we used eye-tracking to investigate how people read a real French poem — Baudelaire’s Les Chats. The linguistic content of the poem never changed. Only its visual presentation did: verse versus prose.

The effect was immediate and measurable. Readers spent more time, reread more often, and processed rhyme differently when the poem appeared in its original verse form.

Nothing about the semantics had changed. What changed was how meaning was experienced.

This kind of result is difficult to detect if we only evaluate output quality or textual plausibility. But it becomes visible when meaning is approached through human behavior: eye movements, hesitation, rereading, attention allocation. Meaning, in this view, is not only semantic content. It emerges from form, sound, layout, expectation, and task.

From eye movements to metaphor and beauty

Later, during my PhD work, I extended this logic to a larger scale.

Instead of a single poem, I worked with an ecological corpus of French literature, from which I constructed a specialized poetry subcorpus. Using NLP tools, I extracted candidate metaphorical phrases based on syntactic patterns, validated them with expert judgments, and tested them in large-scale online studies involving 1,500 participants.

Participants rated short phrases on comprehension, familiarity, beauty, and metaphoricity.

Once again, the goal was not to label metaphors automatically. It was to ask a more fundamental question: which measurable properties of language shape human judgments?

The results were strikingly consistent. Beauty and metaphoricity clustered together, while comprehension and familiarity formed a separate cluster. Judgments were shaped by semantic similarity, sound structure, aesthetic potential, and syntactic form. Familiarity mattered — but not linearly. Extreme familiarity and extreme unfamiliarity led to confident judgments, while intermediate familiarity produced ambiguity and hesitation.

What emerged was a graded picture of meaning. Metaphoricity and beauty were not subjective noise. They were structured responses to identifiable properties of language.

What pre-LLM NLP understood well

Looking back, several principles stand out.

First, meaning was never reduced to a single score. It was decomposed into multiple dimensions — similarity, affect, familiarity, imagery, effort, aesthetics — even when the models themselves were simple.

Second, human evaluation was not optional. Eye movements, reaction times, and ratings were not treated as secondary validation. They were taken as reference, because they reflect actual human behavioral responses to linguistic stimuli. Meaning was inferred from how people read, hesitate, reread, and judge — not from model output alone.

Third, tasks anchored interpretation. A task forced researchers to define success with explicit control and constraints. It made clear what was being tested, under which conditions, and for whom. Without a task, “understanding” quickly became rhetorical rather than empirical.

Finally, interpretability mattered. Features were not only tools for prediction. They were designed to be understood, criticized, and revised. Modeling was as much about insight as it was about performance.

What changed in the post-LLM shift

LLMs are extraordinary, but their success altered our habits.

In many applied settings — demos, prototypes, and parts of the research discourse — explicit tasks are increasingly replaced by prompts, and evaluation drifts toward qualitative impressions. Fluency becomes a proxy for meaning. Aesthetic quality is treated as an emergent property rather than something that can be analyzed.

We gained flexibility and scale. But we lost explicit operational constraints.

Without those constraints, it becomes harder to explain why a system works, harder to distinguish comprehension from engagement, harder to separate beauty from coherence, and harder to notice when models perform well for the wrong reasons.

LLMs can produce metaphors beautifully. But producing language is not the same as modeling the conditions under which humans find something metaphorical or beautiful.

Bigger isn’t always better — it’s often blurrier

Scale has undeniable advantages. But it also has costs.

When everything is included in the training data, genre distinctions soften and signals that matter for specific kinds of language use are averaged out. This is especially problematic for poetry and metaphor, where meaning depends on rare but meaningful patterns.

Metaphor relies on a delicate balance. Expressions must be unfamiliar enough to create surprise, yet familiar enough to be interpretable as metaphorical rather than nonsensical. When language is treated as a homogeneous mass, this balance is blurred. Rare constructions lose their distinctiveness, and the very patterns that carry aesthetic and figurative force are diluted.

For tasks involving interpretation, aesthetic judgment, or expert reading, bigger datasets are not necessarily better datasets. What matters is not coverage, but relevance — relevance to a genre, a reader, a context, and a task.

Pre-LLM NLP made this trade-off explicit. It optimized for alignment rather than sheer volume.

When smaller, specialized corpora made more sense

This difference is especially visible in how data was treated.

Instead of collecting as much text as possible, pre-LLM approaches often relied on highly specific corpora, explicitly aligned with a particular task and a particular human experience. In my own work, this meant starting from a broad literary corpus and then constructing a poetry-focused subcorpus designed to approximate what a reader actually encounters when reading poetry.

The aim was not to model “language in general.” It was to model language as experienced by a reader, in a specific situation, with particular expectations and interpretive habits.

That choice shaped everything downstream. It determined which features were meaningful, which judgments were interpretable, and which questions could even be asked. Familiarity, rarity, sound, and syntactic form mattered precisely because they were evaluated within a constrained and coherent linguistic space.

Meaning was not extracted after the fact. It was encoded into the data design itself.

Why this logic is quietly returning

Interestingly, this task-aligned approach is re-emerging under new names.

Retrieval-augmented generation reintroduces task-specific corpora. Small and local language models favor specialization over generality. On-device systems reduce data circulation and energy costs while prioritizing relevance.

Rather than choosing between handcrafted pipelines and large models, we can now combine curated corpora, explicit task definitions, smaller or local models, and transparent evaluation. This opens the door to systems that are not only fluent, but aligned with specific users and experiences.

Sustainability is part of the equation

There is also an environmental and ethical dimension to this shift.

Running massive models for every query consumes significant energy, centralizes infrastructure, and externalizes environmental cost. Task-specific systems, by contrast, can often run locally, rely on smaller models, and use curated knowledge that is directly relevant to the question being asked.

From this perspective, specialization is not only cognitively sound. It is more sustainable.

Designing systems that know less, but know it better, can be a responsible technical choice.

Meaning lives at the task level

The lesson from pre-LLM NLP is not that large models are wrong.

It is that meaning does not exist independently of tasks.

If we care about understanding, trust, aesthetics, and human experience, we need to keep designing systems where the corpus fits the task, the model fits the context, and evaluation reflects human behavior.

A bridge, not a rejection

This is not an argument against LLMs. It is an argument for continuity.

The future of NLP is not pre-LLM or post-LLM. It is large models combined with well-defined tasks, interpretable features, human evaluation, and respect for how meaning actually shows up in people.

The old questions still matter.
We simply have better tools now — if we choose to use them carefully.
