How sound, familiarity, and semantics shape metaphor judgments

This work comes from a pre-LLM NLP approach combining corpus construction, feature engineering, and large-scale human evaluation.

Evidence from 952 Participants

When people call a phrase “metaphorical,” they often also call it “beautiful.”

This intuition shows up everywhere — in poetry, advertising, and everyday language — but it raises a deeper question:

Are metaphoricity and beauty actually the same judgment, or do they just travel together?

During my PhD, I explored this question using ecologically sourced metaphors, computational linguistic features, and large-scale human ratings. In this article, I’ll focus on what the data revealed when metaphors were presented in isolation, without context.
It is built on earlier peer-reviewed research where I used eye-tracking to study how formal features of French poetry (such as visual layout and rhyme) shape real-time reading behavior, even when textual content remains unchanged.

The experiment in a nutshell

We ran a large online study during the COVID period with 952 French-speaking participants.

Participants rated short phrases extracted from French poetry, selected automatically using syntactic patterns (described in [Article 1]). Each phrase was evaluated on four dimensions:

  • Comprehension
  • Familiarity
  • Beauty
  • Metaphoricity

The material consisted of:

  • 60 metaphorical phrases
  • 60 non-metaphorical phrases

All phrases shared the same syntactic structures (e.g. N de N, N être N, Adj N), allowing us to control form while letting meaning vary naturally.

Two clusters, not four independent judgments

The first striking result was not about metaphors per se — it was about how people use rating scales.

Across participants, the ratings formed two robust clusters:

  • Comprehension ↔ Familiarity
  • Beauty ↔ Metaphoricity

In other words:

  • phrases that were easy to understand were also familiar
  • phrases judged highly metaphorical were often judged beautiful

This pattern appeared consistently, even before any modeling.

This already suggests that metaphoricity is not evaluated purely as a semantic property, but as something that carries aesthetic weight.

Modeling metaphoricity: what actually matters?

To go beyond correlations, we modeled metaphoricity judgments using a combination of:

  • affective–semantic features (e.g. aesthetic potential)
  • sound-based features (e.g. sonority)
  • corpus-derived semantic similarity
  • syntactic pattern type
  • familiarity (modeled non-linearly)

The same predictors emerged repeatedly — whether we modeled: metaphors alone, non-metaphors alone, or the full dataset together.

The strongest contributors were:

  • Aesthetic potential
  • Semantic similarity (distributional, corpus-based)
  • Sonority
  • Syntactic construction
  • A composite affective–aesthetic index adapted from Arthur Jacobs’ neurocognitive poetics framework

Crucially, familiarity mattered only at the extremes: very familiar or very unfamiliar phrases were judged confidently, while mid-range familiarity led to more ambiguity.

A key insight: metaphors and non-metaphors use the same dimensions

One might expect metaphors and non-metaphors to rely on entirely different cues.

They didn’t.

Participants appeared to use the same dimensions — sound, aesthetics, semantic distance, familiarity — for both kinds of phrases. What differed was how strongly those dimensions were weighted.

This supports a graded view of metaphoricity: metaphoricity is not a binary switch it emerges from a combination of interacting properties aesthetic features are part of the signal, not a by-product

Why familiarity behaves strangely (and why that’s interesting)

Familiarity did not show a simple linear effect.

Instead:

  • highly familiar phrases were quickly categorized (often as non-metaphorical)
  • highly unfamiliar phrases were also quickly categorized (often as metaphorical)
  • phrases in between generated hesitation

This pattern aligns with:

  • Relevance Theory (broader interpretation space under uncertainty)
  • foregrounding accounts in literary processing

In other words, effort and ambiguity arise not from novelty alone, but from intermediate predictability.

What this says about beauty

Beauty was not “just noise.”

Many of the same predictors that explained metaphoricity also explained beauty judgments:

  • aesthetic potential
  • sonority
  • semantic relations

This doesn’t mean beauty is metaphoricity — but it suggests that metaphors recruit aesthetic processing early, even when presented without context.

Metaphoricity, in this sense, is not only about meaning transfer. It’s also about how language sounds, feels, and invites interpretation.

Why this matters beyond metaphor studies

These results have broader implications:

  • For psycholinguistics, they support models where figurative meaning emerges from multiple interacting cues.
  • For computational NLP, they highlight the value of interpretable features over single-score metaphor detectors.
  • For aesthetic language research, they show that beauty judgments are not epiphenomenal — they are part of meaning construction.

In a field increasingly dominated by end-to-end models, this kind of work reminds us that understanding language still requires carefully designed tasks and human judgments.

What comes next

In a second study, we examined these same metaphors in context, to test how surrounding text reshapes metaphoricity and beauty judgments.

Context changes many things — but not everything.

That story is for another article.
