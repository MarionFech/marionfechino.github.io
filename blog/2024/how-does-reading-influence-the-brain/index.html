For as long as I can remember, reading fiction has always been my escape from the real world. A place of comfort, curiosity, and high entertainment. First, it was a way to share a moment before bed with my mum. Then, it was a way to empathize with many characters and stories as a playground, a way to live through the characters as a teenager. Even today, with a house to hold, work to achieve, and a baby to care for, I still find myself drowned in the excitement of discovering and enrolling in a new story and forgetting about everything as I dive deep into that universe. The frenzy of a good read always makes me forget everything.

I remember falling deeply in love with many characters, their punchlines, the crazy outcomes and sinuous roads of the scenario, the ‘aha’ feeling when getting beforehand a twist, the puzzle to assemble, the dramatic feeling when following the hero into his quest, the relieve when a character makes it through the adventure, the crushing when a beloved character dies as I felt I lost a friend, the happiness when a particular story finally untangled, and the characters achieve their dream.

I have always had a very personal and intimate feeling about my books. It was a secret conversation between those characters and myself, a personal relationship, something I am the only one to know. It was always a little epiphany for me to understand how that connection was shared by so many readers worldwide, feeling that same personal connection to those characters and that story.

I studied cognitive psychology for many years, and many linguists studied language acquisition or the lack thereof. It makes sense as it is such a key element, specifically in the internet and social media era, influencing children and young readers so much. However, I discovered studies and theories on fictional reading many years later.

I will not address the controversial question of cognitive linguistics about what is natural and what is acquired in language. From a neurolinguistic perspective, reading is a skill that recycles areas of the brain to function. So, to learn to read, we rewind and re-use parts of the brain that were not made for that first.

Moreover, many studies with fMRI and EEG showed that expert readers use a very connected and rich information net when reading a word. When reading a specific word, the brain activates all the information related to past experiences, encounters, feelings, and categorizations in line with the world. Of course, the activation is not strong as it would be too costly and ineffective, but enough to be present. Moreover, the brain even mimics the word action or feeling if it is in the “database.” For example, a verb of action will activate a bit of the motor area in line with the muscles and body parts involved in that particular action. We enact a bit of what we read!

Immersive reading is the key feeling of being lost in a narrative world where we no longer focus on the text but deep-living the adventure as a mental movie. There are lots of different terminologies and theories around that*.

How can we go from affecting an area of the brain for reading to being so efficient in associating a sign with a meaning that we can forget about the technicalities and just focus on the travel?

I would like to discuss a few of them; first, Ryan (2015) introduced a model where she defined different bubbles of realities. First, there is the unattainable mask of the objective reality, and then somewhere overlapping, an individual representation of reality views the reality. Then there is the bubble of a fictional universe where this can overlap or not the reality. When we embrace a story, we take the universe and its code for granted. She states that to do so, we adjust by recentering the circle and adopting the fictional one as ours.

Once we adopt the universe, we use a make-believe mechanism to bring the characters and story alive. As Walton (1990) suggested, the immersion process begins very early in life. Children use props such as dolls, teddy bears, and game plays all the time. It is also a shared skill to understand paintings, films, plays, novels…

However, going from reading and accepting the narrator's rules to totally immersed in a world takes a certain mental effort. Some psychologists call that phenomenon mental simulation (Oatley, 2012). Reading would be seen as a simulation of real-life events. The author states two sides are involved: one with the instructions handled by the text itself and one by the reader, who will imagine and bring the description and its specific experience and culture to life.

The theory of mind is another important skill at the core of understanding and feeling the story. To feel the story from the character's point of view, we need to be able to put our own feelings and emotions aside and put ourselves in someone else's shoes, as is the case when we want to interact with other people and understand them.

“We take our own decision-making ‘off-line’.” (Stich and Nichols, 2000)

There is even more at stake when reading. We can adapt and feel the point of view of a character that is totally stranger to us. Usually, fiction describes in detail feelings, sometimes even internal discussions and options when a character is faced with big challenges. It is the opportunity to deepen someone else's view and explore options we would usually not even consider or condemn. It is a way to bring closer the thinking processes of people we would never agree with in reality, a way to feel a bigger leap of tolerance towards differences. It is also a way to open more roads of thinking and different argumentation processes. There is a richness when reading as to enter more deeply into the intimate motivation and argumentation of the characters, characters that we would usually not consider because they are far away from our beliefs, culture, time, etc.

There is also a neurological basis to that mental simulation. There is a part in the pre-motor cortex where mirror neurons will be activated either if we do an action or are watching someone else doing an action (Marr, 1982/2010). This activity is not enough for us to do the movement, but it could be the basis of the theory of mind and empathy to understand others and a way to put ourselves in someone else's shoes. When reading a book, readers in an fMRI study showed that same activity as if they saw someone else doing the actions.

“When the subjects read about the hero interacting with an object, brain activity increased in the areas (frontal lobes) known to control grasping motions. When the subjects read about character movements through the story world, brain activity increased in areas known to be activated when people view pictures of spatial scenes. This suggests a mapping activity. The signals produced by the subjects were different from the signals produced when reading isolated words. Furthermore, certain regions of the brain were activated only when the state of the story world underwent multiple changes between sentences. This suggests that modelization requires a more intense effort.” (Speer et al., 2009)

Many studies still need to be done to explore how the brain can grasp and catch reading depth. Do we also simulate the taste and smell? The sound? From an occam razor point of view, it is possible to re-enact all past experiences stored in our memory and combine them to access a multisensorial mental simulation.

Immersion is still partly a mystery as it is still early to understand what triggers that flow. The reader-text-context interaction (Dixon et al, 1993) could be a good start to understanding the mechanisms underpinning it. However, it seems certain that this immersion, the associated flow feeling, and how we are deeply committed to the story take multiple brain processes to grasp the scenario fully at play.

In conclusion, reading is far from passive; it is very nurturing, instructive, and emotionally bonding. And if we have the time, we should all enjoy a nice piece of fiction; it will always be good training for our brains! It is like meeting someone else, going on an adventure, and discovering new ways of thinking and doing things. Our brain will be richer from the virtual experiment as each time a path is activated, it is reinforced! (101 basic neurobiology).

So read, read anything, enjoy, train your brain.

*(from Ryan, 2024 in James, Kubo, and Lavocat, 2024) “Jean-Marie Schaeffer, in his 1999 book Pourquoi la fiction? Yet Walton’s ambition to explain the experience of “being caught in a story” (1990) anticipates current interest in the phenomenon of immersion. Other metaphors under which immersion (itself a metaphor) has been studied are “being lost in a book” (Nell 1988), “entrancement” (also Nell), and “transportation” (Gerrig 1993).
